## 2026 ACQUISITIONS — CRINETICS PHARMACEUTICALS

**CHINMAY**
So. July 6th, Vertex agrees to buy Crinetics for eighty-five dollars a share in cash. About ten billion on the equity, eight point eight billion net of the cash Crinetics is sitting on. For context, the biggest thing Vertex had ever done before this was Alpine Immune Sciences in 2024 at four point nine billion. So this is double their record.

What they're buying is two assets. PALSONIFY, an oral drug for acromegaly, FDA approved in September of last year. And atumelnant, which is in Phase 3 for congenital adrenal hyperplasia. Vertex is guiding to roughly five billion dollars in combined peak annual sales, and strategically this stands endocrinology up as a whole new pillar for them, alongside cystic fibrosis, pain, blood disorders and kidney.

The launch is going well, by the way. PALSONIFY did about five million dollars in Q4 last year, ten point three million in Q1, twenty-four million in Q2. Shareholders approved the deal on Friday, and it's expected to close on or about September 1st.

Issa — you told me before we started this was the weirdest good deal you've looked at all year. Why?

**ISSA**
Because of how the price got set. And you only find this if you read the background-of-the-merger section in the proxy, which is maybe six pages and is the most interesting thing anybody filed this summer.

Vertex opens at seventy-eight a share in late March. Board turns it down. Vertex comes back in April at eighty-three. Board turns that down too, and tells their bankers, fine, go find out who else is out there. So they line up six potential counterparties. One of them — the proxy calls them Party A — actually digs in, and then around the first of May they walk, because they can't get themselves north of six billion dollars. The other two still circling drop out the following week.

So now there's no auction. There's no second bidder. There's nobody to leak to. And Vertex raises their own bid anyway. Eighty-four fifty. Crinetics counters at eighty-seven, Vertex says eighty-five, best and final, board takes it in June. They bid against themselves. Twice.

And eighty-five is a hundred and two percent premium. I can put that in context, because we benchmark this in Solidus, which is our deal intelligence platform — fifty to a hundred percent is the normal premium band across acquisitions. So a hundred and two isn't off the map. It's the ceiling of the map. It's what buyers pay when they *do* have competition.

Now, people are going to hear "sole bidder, huge premium" and jump straight to "they overpaid," and that's lazy. When there's no auction the price isn't telling you what the asset's worth on the open market. It's telling you what it's worth to Vertex specifically. Different numbers.

And what it's worth to Vertex is about duration. Look at what they actually bought — annualize that twenty-four million dollar quarter and you're at, call it a hundred million a year, against eight point eight billion. That's not a revenue multiple anybody's underwriting. And the other half of the thesis, atumelnant, is a Phase 3 asset. First patient dosed in the pivotal adult trial in December. That is not de-risked. So if the five billion peak is real, two times peak is a normal multiple — Stifel and William Blair both landed roughly there, rich but defensible *if* the peak happens. What I'd point at is Vertex's own guidance: accretive to non-GAAP operating income in 2029. Three years out. Nobody writes a ten billion dollar check for something that starts paying in three years unless what they're actually worried about is what their revenue looks like in the 2030s. This is a patent cliff purchase. It's just not being described that way.

**CHINMAY**
Let's do structure, because there's a lot in the filings.

It's all cash. No stock, no contingent value rights, no earnout of any kind. On financing — Vertex went into this with thirteen point six billion in cash and marketable securities and zero long-term debt. On announcement day they lined up four and a half billion of committed bridge financing from Bank of America and Morgan Stanley, then on July 30th they killed the bridge and replaced it with an actual term loan. Four and a half billion, senior unsecured, delayed draw, three tranches — a billion due in 364 days, a billion at two years, two and a half billion at three years. They hadn't drawn a dollar as of the last 10-Q.

On the accounting, worth noting Alpine in 2024 got structured as an asset acquisition, which meant four point four billion went straight through the income statement in one quarter as acquired in-process R&D.

Regulatory — HSR waiting period ran out mid-August, no second request, and they've cleared Austria, Germany and Australia. Top-tier filing fee, two point four six million dollars.

And on Friday's vote, there's a split. The merger passed with seventy-nine point two million shares for and thirteen thousand six hundred against. But the advisory vote on golden parachute compensation failed — about thirty-four million for, forty-five million against.

What do you make of all that?

**ISSA**
Four things, and they're all doing something.

Start with the CVR, or the fact that there isn't one. A CVR is the bet you bolt onto a deal when the two sides can't agree — buyer thinks the drug's a coin flip, seller thinks it's a sure thing, so you say fine, you get X now and another Y if it gets approved. And there was an *obvious* one to write here. Atumelnant. Put a CVR on the CAH readout and everybody goes home happy. That's textbook. They didn't do it, and they paid a hundred and two percent premium on top of not doing it. Solidus has sixteen hundred-plus verified transactions in it going back to 2017, all pulled from SEC EDGAR, HSR filings, ClinicalTrials.gov — filings, nothing scraped — and the pattern is dead consistent. Contingent consideration shows up exactly where the two sides are arguing about probability. So a buyer skipping it in a spot where it's begging to be used is a buyer saying: we're not arguing, we think we know.

Second, the debt. Vertex has never really been a borrower, and now they're doing their first big one. But look at the shape of that ladder — 364 days, two years, three years. They've built themselves a runway to pay the whole thing off out of operating cash flow. That's not a company that wants to be levered. That's a company borrowing toward a specific date and planning to be done.

Third, and this one's going to fool people. Alpine torched the GAAP numbers in a single quarter because of how it was structured. Crinetics is a different animal — there's an approved product and a real commercial organization attached — so I'd expect this one to get treated as a business combination instead. Goodwill and intangibles capitalized, amortized over years, no single-quarter crater. Same money out the door, completely different-looking financials. Vertex hasn't confirmed that, to be clear, that's my read. But if you sit there comparing their GAAP earnings across 2024, 2026 and 2027 without adjusting for it, you will draw the exact wrong conclusion about that company.

Fourth, the regulatory bit, and there's a wrinkle nobody's covering. Two years ago the FTC rewrote the HSR form — first real overhaul since 1978, way more documents, and the whole deal bar spent last year complaining about the workload. This past February a federal court in East Texas vacated it. Threw the rule out. FTC went to the Fifth Circuit for a stay, Fifth Circuit said no. So the agencies are back on the old form, and the biggest biopharma deal of the year filed on the pre-2025 paperwork. Fee's still two point four six million, but the burden was a fraction of what it would've been twelve months ago. And that window may close — FTC's appealing.

Oh — and the parachute vote. Ninety-nine point nine eight percent of votes cast said yes to eighty-five dollars in cash. Same ballot, same shareholders, said no to what management is collecting on the way out. It's non-binding, doesn't touch the closing, and most people will scroll past it. I wouldn't. Usually means the change-of-control package got richer faster than the stock did. And it's a reminder for every founder listening that while your bankers are negotiating the deal, your comp committee is negotiating a second one, and your shareholders can see both.

## 2026 LICENSINGS — HALOZYME THERAPEUTICS

**CHINMAY**
Completely different posture on the licensing side.

April 7th this year, Vertex signs a global exclusive collaboration and license with Halozyme for their Hypercon technology, covering up to three drug targets. Fifteen million dollars upfront, plus future milestones and royalties on net sales.

Hypercon is a microparticle platform — it lets you hyperconcentrate a biologic so you deliver the same dose in a much smaller injection volume. Which is what makes convenient at-home dosing possible instead of sending patients to an infusion center. Halozyme's been clear it complements ENHANZE rather than replacing it.

Fifteen million against a ten billion dollar acquisition in the same year. Is that a rounding error or is it strategic?

**ISSA**
It's both, and that's the whole point of a deal like this.

Here's what I love about the number. Fifteen million is *exactly* the median upfront for a discovery-stage deal in our data. Dead on the median. So Vertex paid the going rate for one preclinical program and got a platform across three targets for it.

And structurally, what you're buying isn't the technology. You're buying the right to *use* the technology, at a price low enough that you don't have to be correct. All the real money is pushed out into milestones and royalties, which means Halozyme only gets paid if Vertex actually succeeds with it. Risk sits with the party who controls the outcome. That's how these should be built and a lot of them aren't.

Now contrast that with Crinetics, where Vertex took every ounce of risk onto their own balance sheet on day one. Same company, same twelve months, completely opposite posture. And both are right, because the two assets are at opposite ends of how much anybody actually knows.

That's not just a Vertex thing either. Option structures were about eight percent of the transactions in our set back in 2022. They're around eighteen percent now. More than doubled in four years. Everybody's worked out you can rent conviction instead of buying it.

**CHINMAY**
What does it tell you about where Vertex is headed? Because they're a small molecule house, historically. Cystic fibrosis, the pain program — that's chemistry, that's pills.

**ISSA**
Right, and that's exactly the tell. You don't go license high-concentration *biologics* delivery for a portfolio you don't have.

Povetacicept's a fusion protein. So read it forward — this is a company building the plumbing for a future portfolio that's injectable, chronic, and self-administered at home. Subcutaneous conversion is one of the more reliably value-accretive moves in this industry, because you get three things at once: better adherence, a defensible follow-on patent position, and a switching story that protects you the day a biosimilar turns up.

And they bought that option for fifteen million dollars, years before they need it, on a platform that sits alongside what they might already use rather than replacing it. That's about the cheapest strategic option anybody bought this year.

## 2025 LICENSINGS — ONO PHARMACEUTICAL

**CHINMAY**
Back to last year for the next two, and they're the same molecule.

June 23rd, 2025 — Vertex and Ono Pharmaceutical sign an exclusive license and collaboration for povetacicept in Japan and South Korea. Povetacicept is a dual BAFF/APRIL antagonist being developed for IgA nephropathy and other B-cell mediated diseases. Ono helps advance the clinical work, takes responsibility for marketing authorizations in both countries, and commercializes there after approval.

And note the direction of the money — Vertex is the one receiving. Upfront payment, regulatory and commercial milestones, tiered royalties. Terms weren't disclosed.

Why give the territory away at all?

**ISSA**
They're not giving it away, they're renting out rooms they were never going to live in. But you have to remember where this molecule came from to see it properly.

Vertex paid four point nine billion dollars for Alpine Immune Sciences in 2024, and povetacicept *was* that deal. It's the whole asset. So the question in front of them afterward is: what do we do with the seventy percent of the world where we have no infrastructure?

Vertex was never building a nephrology field force in Japan. Never. So the real choice is leave the territory sitting there doing nothing, or convert it into cash coming in the door plus a royalty stream, and keep the US and Europe where almost all the value sits anyway. That's not retreat. That's portfolio management, and I'd hate for anyone to read it the other way.

**CHINMAY**
Why Japan specifically, though? Feels like a small market for a kidney drug. And can you actually put a number on undisclosed terms?

**ISSA**
On Japan — it's the opposite of small, and this is a great bit of trivia. Japan runs mandatory urinalysis screening through the school system, has for decades, and the literature attributes something like seventy to eighty percent of detected mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis to that program. So Japan finds IgA nephropathy earlier and more systematically than basically anywhere on earth. Patients already identified, nephrologists concentrated, and Ono can reach them. That's arguably the cleanest single market in the world for this drug.

On putting a number to it — no. Not honestly, and I want to be straight about that. Our Phase 3 licensing benchmark is a hundred and fifty million median on the upfront, but that's for *global* rights. Ono got two countries. Regional deals price way underneath the global comps, and anybody quoting you the global median for a Japan-and-Korea license is selling you something.

What the benchmarks do tell you is the shape. Milestones run sixty to seventy-five percent of total potential deal value now, and the ratio of total value to upfront has stretched from about eight-to-ten times in 2022 to ten-to-fourteen times today. So: modest upfront, long milestone tail. Which is the right structure, honestly, when your partner is absorbing development and regulatory risk in their own market. You let them pay you as they de-risk it. Ono's buying a shot on goal. Vertex is selling square footage it wasn't using.

## 2025 LICENSINGS — ZAI LAB

**CHINMAY**
And the other one actually came first. January 10th, 2025 — Vertex and Zai Lab, exclusive collaboration and license for povetacicept across mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan and Singapore. Same structure as the Ono deal: Vertex receives an upfront, regulatory milestones, tiered royalties. Terms again undisclosed.

Is China actually a big enough market to matter here, or is this a nice-to-have?

**ISSA**
For IgA nephropathy it's the biggest one there is. China carries the largest absolute patient population in the world, and it's the most common biopsy-proven glomerulonephritis there by a wide margin. This is not a nice-to-have.

But notice which direction the deal points, because that's the part I'd flag. For three years the entire story in this industry has been Western pharma *in*-licensing Chinese assets. Cheap molecules, fast enrollment, NewCo structures — it's all anyone talks about at JPM. This is the opposite. A Western company out-licensing into China. That's rarer than people think, and I suspect we see more of it.

And Zai Lab's become sort of the default counterparty for this trade, because they've built regulatory and commercial infrastructure that a US biotech genuinely cannot replicate on any timeline that matters. You're not buying a distributor. You're buying five years you don't have.

**CHINMAY**
Put Ono and Zai Lab together for me. What's the pattern?

**ISSA**
Don't read them as two deals. Read them as one move.

In about five months Vertex regionalized an entire acquired asset — Greater China, Singapore, Japan, Korea — while keeping the United States and Europe outright.

So here's the model, and I really want founders to hear this one. Buy the global asset. Keep the two markets that carry the economics. Monetize everything else for cash, speed and local execution. Every one of those regional deals quietly chips away at what Alpine actually cost them, and they gave up nothing in the core to do it.

When a company does that twice inside a year with the same molecule, that's not opportunism. There's a memo behind that.

## THE CLOSEOUT

**ISSA**
So the thread across all four of these is that Vertex is unusually disciplined about where they'll put principal at risk.

They wrote one enormous check — all cash, no contingency, ceiling-of-the-band premium, no competing bidder — for the one thing they had total internal conviction about. And everywhere the conviction was lower, they refused to risk capital. Fifteen million for the Halozyme option. And on the two Asia deals they weren't paying at all, they were collecting.

Cash where they're sure, structure where they're not. And in a year with fifty-plus deals that's rarer than it should be, because a lot of buyers do it backwards — they hedge the thing they're certain about and go all in on the thing they're guessing at.

**CHINMAY**
What are you watching from here?

**ISSA**
Three things. The first post-close quarter, mostly to see how they book it, because that one accounting decision determines whether Vertex's GAAP numbers are comparable to anything for the next two years. The term loan — watch when they draw it and how fast they kill that 364-day tranche, that tells you how confident they really are about cash generation. And whether endocrinology becomes a program or stays a one-off. One deal's a pillar. Two deals is a strategy.

And if you're a founder listening to this trying to work out where you sit in all of it, the answer's in the comps and I mean that literally. Which buyers actually paid cash. Where CVRs showed up and what triggered them. What the premium looks like when there's a real auction versus one bidder in the room. What upfront-to-total runs at your stage, in your indication, for your modality.

That's the whole reason we built Solidus. Every number I've quoted today came out of it and every one of them traces back to a filing. It's at solidus dot ambrosiaventures dot co, and it's free to start. And the reason it matters — upfronts are up twenty to thirty percent across every stage since 2022. If you're negotiating off a comp you remember from a few years ago, you're negotiating against yourself.

**CHINMAY**
Vertex just told us what conviction costs, and what it's worth not to have to guess. Thanks Issa.

Catch us on the next segment of Hot Off The Press.
